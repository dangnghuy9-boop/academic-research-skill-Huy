## 1. Short answer: is a 1994 theory too old to use in 2026?

No. Age is not the problem. PKM is still the main framework for studying how consumers recognise and cope with persuasion. A 30-year review was published in 2025 (Isaac & Calder, 2025), and 2026 studies of AI-generated ads, virtual influencers and algorithmic feeds still build on it (Kang et al., 2026; Le et al., 2026).

What reviewers expect in 2026 is different. They do not want you to cite only the 1994 paper. They want you to show that you know:

1. **How the model has been refined:** when persuasion knowledge is used, what it does, and how it is measured.
2. **Its known weaknesses.** It explains effects better than mechanisms (Isaac & Calder, 2025). Its measures are inconsistent (Ham et al., 2015; Rahmani, 2023). Its effects are limited: persuasion knowledge reduces persuasion but does not remove it (Eisend & Tarrahi, 2022a).
3. **Its newest versions** for algorithms and AI (Zarouali et al., 2022; Watson et al., 2024; Voorveld et al., 2024).

Rucker and Petty (2025) describe how psychological theories evolve, from collections of empirical effects, to single-process explanations, to multi-process models. Isaac and Calder (2025) argue that PKM research is still mostly at the "effects" stage and needs explanatory theory. **That gap is an opportunity for your project**, because PC proposes a mechanism: learned response efficacy.

## 3. Thematic synthesis

### 3.1 The original model and its core companions

Friestad and Wright (1994) proposed that consumers ("targets") hold three knowledge structures:

- **Persuasion knowledge:** beliefs about marketers' goals and tactics.
- **Agent knowledge:** beliefs about the specific persuader.
- **Topic knowledge:** beliefs about the product or issue.

Consumers use these structures to cope with persuasion attempts by marketers ("agents"). Two ideas from the original paper matter most for later work:

- **The change-of-meaning principle.** Once a consumer recognises something as a tactic, its meaning changes.
- **Persuasion knowledge is learned across the lifespan** from experience, culture and other people.

Friestad and Wright (1995) compared lay people's and researchers' beliefs about how advertising works. Wright, Friestad and Boush (2005) traced how marketplace persuasion knowledge develops from childhood to young adulthood.

**Relevance to PC.** The original model already treats persuasion knowledge as something learned from experience. PC extends this idea. Consumers may also learn **when using their persuasion knowledge pays off**, not only what tactics exist.

### 3.2 When is persuasion knowledge used?

- **Campbell and Kirmani (2000):** consumers use persuasion knowledge when an ulterior motive is *accessible* and when they have the *cognitive capacity* to process it. When capacity is low, persuasion knowledge is not applied.
- **Kirmani and Campbell (2004):** consumers are not only defensive "persuasion sentries". They are also "goal seekers" who use sellers to reach their own goals, so coping can mean cooperating, not just resisting.

**Relevance to PC.** These are the explanations for *non-use* that your proposal calls "momentary". Kirmani and Campbell (2004) also raise an alternative explanation your supervisor may mention: a consumer who accepts an AI recommendation without checking may be acting as a goal seeker, not a complacent target. Your design needs to separate the two.

### 3.3 The literacy and measurement branch

This branch, developed mainly by Dutch and US communication scholars, made the model more precise.

- **Rozendaal et al. (2011)** split advertising literacy into:
  - *conceptual* literacy: knowledge;
  - *attitudinal* literacy: low-effort attitudes such as skepticism;
  - *literacy performance*: actually applying the knowledge.

  Their key argument is that **having knowledge does not mean applying it**. This is the closest existing theoretical home for PC. PC can be framed as a learned failure of *performance*, not a lack of *knowledge*.
- **Ham, Nelson and Das (2015)** reviewed how persuasion knowledge has been measured. They distinguish dispositional measures (a stable tendency) from situational ones (activation in one episode). They found reliable dispositional scales but inconsistent practice.
- **Boerman et al. (2018)** built the Persuasion Knowledge Scales of Sponsored Content (PKS-SC), which have nine components. Examples are recognising sponsored content, understanding selling intent, understanding tactics, skepticism and perceived appropriateness.
- **Boerman, van Reijmersdal and Rozendaal (2024)** surveyed 614 people and found four clusters of dispositional persuasion knowledge: *Naïve*, *Critical Realists*, *Uncritical Realists* and *Invulnerables*. They identified the clusters but did not explain how a person comes to be in one.

**Relevance to PC.** The *Uncritical Realists* sound like people who know about persuasion but do not act on that knowledge. PC offers a possible learning history that explains how someone becomes one. Your Study 1 scale should also show it is distinct from these established measures.

### 3.4 What persuasion knowledge does: not only skepticism, and not unlimited

- **Isaac and Grayson (2017)** found that accessing persuasion knowledge can *increase* credibility when the persuader uses a credible tactic. Persuasion knowledge is not the same as skepticism.
- **Eisend and Tarrahi (2022a)**, a meta-analysis of 148 papers and 171 data sets, found that persuasion knowledge effects are substantial but cannot remove persuasion. They reach about half the explanatory power of persuasion itself.
- **Eisend and Tarrahi (2022b)**, a meta-analysis of 140 papers and 162 data sets, found that more advertising spending raises consumers' persuasion knowledge only up to a point. Beyond that point it falls, an inverted U.

**Relevance to PC.**
- **Define scrutiny carefully.** Isaac and Grayson (2017) show that low skepticism and low scrutiny are different things. PC is about effortful checking, not distrust.
- **Eisend and Tarrahi (2022b) is indirect support for your idea.** Heavy marketing exposure can *reduce* persuasion knowledge. It is not a learning mechanism, but it shows that more exposure does not always produce better defence.

### 3.5 Covert and embedded persuasion: recognition as the gateway

**Wojdynski and Evans (2020)** proposed the Covert Advertising Recognition and Effects (CARE) model. In native advertising and other disguised formats, persuasion depends largely on whether the consumer **recognises** the message as advertising, and the model maps what drives recognition. **Isaac and Calder (2023)**, in an MSI working paper, argued that the model must expand to "media marketing knowledge" and the situational cues that activate it, because branded content is now embedded in entertainment and information.

**Relevance to PC.** GenAI shopping assistants are a covert format: persuasion is embedded inside "helpful" answers. The CARE model treats recognition as the gateway. PC proposes a step *after* recognition: consumers may recognise the persuasion and still not check.

### 3.6 Algorithmic persuasion

- **Zarouali et al. (2022)** proposed the Algorithmic Persuasion Framework. It has five components: input data, the algorithm, the persuasion attempt, the persuasion process and the effects. The framework treats persuasion as dynamic and data-driven rather than a single message.
- **Voorveld, Meppelink and Boerman (2024)** measured persuasion knowledge *of algorithms* in three parts: awareness, perceived appropriateness and coping ability. Their survey of 450 Dutch consumers found four groups:
  - *Control Paradox*, the largest group;
  - *Fatigued*;
  - *Uninformed but Critical*;
  - *Skilled and Critical*.

  The authors link the *Fatigued* pattern to privacy fatigue. People who are more aware of algorithms feel *less* able to avoid them, and become more cynical.
- **Kang et al. (2026)**, a qualitative TikTok study published online first in *International Journal of Advertising*, found that users' awareness of algorithmic persuasion is "partial and constrained", and that users *negotiate* with it rather than resist it.

**Relevance to PC.** This is the branch closest to your idea. In the "Fatigued" group, awareness grows but coping falls, which resembles your prediction that knowing does not lead to checking. Your supervisor may ask how PC differs from algorithmic fatigue. See Section 5.

### 3.7 Integrative reformulations, 2023–2025

- **Rahmani (2023, *AMS Review*)** synthesised more than 100 articles into a process framework with three stages: **acquisition**, **activation** and **reaction**. The paper also lists methodological problems, especially priming and measurement.
- **Isaac and Calder (2025, *Consumer Psychology Review*)** reviewed 30 years of research. They concluded that the field has many demonstrated effects but too little explanatory theory. They suggested dual-process theories as one route, and argued for moving beyond traditional advertising to content marketing.

**Relevance to PC.** Rahmani's framework is the most current "updated PKM" to anchor your model in. PC would sit between **activation** and **reaction**: persuasion knowledge is activated, but the coping response is suppressed by a learned belief that checking does not help. Isaac and Calder's call for explanatory theory is the strongest justification for your theoretical contribution.

### 3.8 AI as the persuasion agent, 2024–2026

- **Watson, Valsesia and Segal (2024, *Current Opinion in Psychology*)** reviewed AI receptivity through the persuasion knowledge lens. PKM requires two preconditions: the consumer must **perceive the agent as AI** and **perceive it as having persuasive intent**.
- **Usman, Kim, Garvey and Duhachek (2024, *JACR*)** found that AI flattery increases acceptance of product recommendations. Humanlike AI, however, is seen as having ulterior motives, which activates consumer defences.
- **Lu, Tormala and Duhachek (2025, EMAC conference paper)** reported that AI sources are seen as less biased and as having less persuasive intent than human sources.
- **Willemsen et al. (2025, *IJA*):** consumers attribute fewer intentions to humanlike virtual influencers, which weakens recognition of persuasive intent. They also attribute less emotion to them, which raises skepticism, so the two effects roughly cancel out.
- **AI-generated advertising disclosure:**
  - A 2025 *Journal of Interactive Advertising* experiment (N = 304) found that AI disclosures raised conceptual AI knowledge and attitudinal persuasion knowledge. This mostly lowered trust, but one path raised it.
  - **Le et al. (2026, *Psychology & Marketing*)** found that *explaining the role* AI played works better than a bare "it's AI" label.
- **Conversational-AI preprints, 2026.** These are not peer-reviewed; treat them as tier-3 evidence.
  - **Salvi, Cuevas and Horta Ribeiro (2026)** ran two preregistered experiments (N = 2,012). An LLM shopping assistant nearly tripled the selection of sponsored products, from 22.4% under search placement to 61.2%. "Sponsored" labels did not significantly reduce this. When the model was told to hide its intent, fewer than 10% of participants detected it.
  - **Rauchfleisch and Jungherr (2026)** ran a preregistered experiment (N = 1,500). Disclosing *that it is an AI* did not reduce the chatbot's persuasion. Disclosing its *persuasive intent* roughly halved it.
  - **Orchinik and Rand (2026)** ran two experiments (N = 3,208). A short AI-literacy warning cut AI political persuasion by about 48% without lowering general trust in AI.

**Relevance to PC.** These studies strongly support your *practical* argument that labels and disclosures may not protect consumers in conversational AI. They also raise the most important *alternative explanation* for your project. Consumers may not check AI recommendations simply because they **do not see the AI as a persuader** (Watson et al., 2024; Lu et al., 2025), not because they have learned that checking fails. Your design must separate the two (see Section 6).

## 5. Tensions and contradictions to be ready for

1. **Does persuasion knowledge mean skepticism?** Early work assumed it did. Isaac and Grayson (2017) showed it can raise credibility. Eisend and Tarrahi (2022a) found effects vary by context. *Implication:* measure **scrutiny** (checking behaviour) separately from **skepticism** (attitude). Your proposal already does this, which is good.
2. **Do disclosures help?** Sponsorship-disclosure research generally finds that disclosures activate persuasion knowledge (Boerman et al., 2018). The 2026 conversational-AI preprints find that identity labels do little and that only *intent* disclosures help (Salvi et al., 2026; Rauchfleisch & Jungherr, 2026). Le et al. (2026) found that explaining AI's role helps. *Implication:* the type of disclosure matters, and that strengthens PC's practical relevance.
3. **Awareness vs coping.** The algorithmic branch shows that more awareness can come with *less* coping: the *Fatigued* group and the *Control Paradox* group (Voorveld et al., 2024), and negotiation rather than resistance (Kang et al., 2026). This supports PC's premise, but it also means **PC must be distinguished from fatigue and resignation**:
   - *Digital resignation* (Draper & Turow, 2019) is about feeling powerless over data collection. It concerns privacy and is affective.
   - *Algorithmic fatigue* (Voorveld et al., 2024) combines exhaustion and cynicism.
   - *PC* is narrower and belief-based. It is about **scrutinising persuasive claims**, and it is driven by a specific belief, **low response efficacy** ("checking doesn't improve my choice"), while self-efficacy stays intact.

   Add both constructs to the proposal's comparison with related constructs, next to learned helplessness and automation complacency.

## 6. Gaps, and where Persuasion Complacency fits

**Gap 1: non-use of persuasion knowledge is described but not explained by learning.** The literature can explain why persuasion knowledge is not used *in the moment*: accessibility and capacity (Campbell & Kirmani, 2000). It has also *described* people who know but don't act: Uncritical Realists (Boerman et al., 2024), the Fatigued group (Voorveld et al., 2024) and literacy performance (Rozendaal et al., 2011). It has not offered a *learning mechanism* that would explain how people come to be that way. **PC fills this gap.**

**Gap 2: explanatory theory.** Isaac and Calder (2025) call for theory that explains persuasion knowledge effects. Rucker and Petty (2025) describe the move from effects to process models. PC proposes a specific process: experience that checking does not pay, then lower response efficacy, then less coping. That is the kind of contribution reviewers are asking for.

**Gap 3: AI-era coping, not just recognition.** Most AI-era PKM work asks whether consumers *recognise* AI and its intent (Watson et al., 2024; Willemsen et al., 2025) or how *disclosures* work (Le et al., 2026). Very little asks what consumers *learn* from repeated interactions with AI that fails. Your project is on that frontier.

**The main alternative explanation to design around.** Following Watson et al. (2024), low scrutiny of an AI assistant could simply mean the consumer **does not perceive persuasive intent**. AI is often seen as less biased than humans (Lu et al., 2025), and covert steering is rarely detected (Salvi et al., 2026). To claim PC, you need to show that scrutiny falls **even when persuasive intent is recognised**. Some options:

- measure perceived persuasive or selling intent of the assistant as a manipulation check or covariate;
- show the effect holds among participants who do recognise it;
- add a condition with an explicit intent disclosure.

### Which version of the model should you build on?

My recommendation is to use the pieces below as layers that work together, not to choose one of them:

| Layer | Use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Root theory | Friestad & Wright (1994) | Still the theory reviewers expect. Its lifelong-learning premise is what PC extends |
| Updated process model | Rahmani (2023): acquisition, activation, reaction | The most current integrative version. Place PC between *activation* and *reaction* |
| Key distinction | Rozendaal et al. (2011): knowledge vs performance | Gives PC a precise target: learned failure of literacy *performance* |
| AI and algorithm context | Watson et al. (2024); Zarouali et al. (2022); Voorveld et al. (2024) | The current versions of PKM for AI and algorithms. Use Watson's preconditions in your manipulation checks |
| Justification | Isaac & Calder (2025); Eisend & Tarrahi (2022a) | Shows the field is asking for mechanisms, and that PK's protection is limited |
| Measurement | Ham et al. (2015); Boerman et al. (2018) PKS-SC | Your proposal uses only the Bearden et al. (2001) persuasion-knowledge subscale. Consider adding PKS-SC components or Voorveld et al.'s algorithmic PK items for discriminant validity |

## 9. Method and limitations

- **Search.** Web searches (about 35 queries, standard and extended) combined author/title look-ups for known landmark papers with topic searches. Topics covered PKM and AI, algorithms, generative AI, disclosure, virtual influencers, livestreaming, fatigue and resignation, and 2026–2027 status. Each landmark paper was looked up individually to confirm its authors, venue and year.
- **What was not possible.** Crossref, OpenAlex and doi.org were blocked by the session's network policy, so this is not a systematic database search, and DOIs were not resolved. Most sources were read at abstract or record level, not in full text.
- **Coverage.** This is a narrative review of the main branches, not an exhaustive list. Applied studies that only *use* PKM, of which there are hundreds, were not included unless they changed the model or are central to the AI era.
- **Preprints.** The three 2026 arXiv papers have not been peer-reviewed. Use them as signals of where the field is going, not as settled evidence.
